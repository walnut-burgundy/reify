# Fox story fixtures: tidy and untidy

These are ordinary-language passages for substitution and transformation experiments. The first few are deliberately easy. After that, the corpus becomes less cooperative.

The point is not that every occurrence of `fox` has one mechanically determined replacement. Sometimes the fox is a predator, sometimes prey, sometimes a scavenger, sometimes a parent, sometimes an observer, sometimes a trickster in a fable, and sometimes merely one participant in a relation that has no useful predator/prey analogue.

A checked `sed` should therefore be allowed to say:

```text
clean transformation
partial transformation
several plausible transformations
cannot justify this transformation from the declared mapping
```

rather than forcing every passage into the same little model.

## Very tidy chicken cases

A red fox waited in the weeds beyond the chicken coop. Near dusk, one chicken slipped through a loose board and wandered toward the fence. The fox rushed across the grass, caught the chicken, and ate it beneath the hedge.

---

Three chickens scratched for beetles behind a barn while a fox watched from the edge of the field. The farmer scared the fox away once, but it circled back after dark. By morning one chicken was gone; the fox had eaten it.

---

A fox found a narrow gap under the wire around the henhouse. It squeezed through without waking the dog and crept toward the roost. One chicken fluttered down, the fox caught it, and the fox ate the chicken before escaping through the same gap.

---

The chickens usually stayed close to the shed because a fox had been seen near the woods. During a storm, a gate blew open and one chicken wandered into the orchard. The fox found the chicken there and ate it.

---

At sunrise a farmer counted twelve chickens in the yard. A fox was hiding beside the stone wall. When the flock scattered, the fox chased one chicken into the tall grass, killed it, and ate the chicken.

## Still predator/prey, but not the same sentence

A fox stood motionless in a snowy field, listening. It jumped almost straight upward and came down nose-first through the crust. A moment later it pulled out a vole and swallowed it.

---

A rabbit broke from the grass and ran along the fence. The fox chased it almost to the woodpile, but the rabbit went through a gap too small for the fox. Nothing was eaten.

---

The vixen caught a young rabbit near the ditch. Instead of eating it there, she carried it back toward the den. Two kits came out to meet her.

---

A fox dug beneath a patch of leaves and uncovered a mouse it had hidden the night before. The mouse was already dead. The fox ate half and buried the rest somewhere else.

---

A ground-nesting bird flew up suddenly from the weeds. The fox did not catch the bird, but it found the nest and ate two eggs. The bird circled overhead calling until the fox left.

---

A fox spent ten minutes snapping at grasshoppers along the edge of a field. It caught several insects, missed most of them, and eventually wandered off after hearing a mouse in the grass.

---

A fox found a dead fish beside a shrinking pond and carried it away. Nothing in the story says that the fox caught or killed the fish.

---

A deer had been dead beside the road since morning. That night a fox scavenged from the carcass. Replacing `deer` with an ordinary prey species would preserve the word `eat` while quietly changing the causal story.

---

The orchard floor was covered with fallen apples. The fox ate fruit for several minutes while rabbits fed on clover at the other end of the field. It never approached them.

---

A fox nosed through blackberry canes, ate ripe berries, then pounced on a beetle. Calling one noun the fox's `prey` would throw away half the sentence.

## Fable-shaped interactions

These are paraphrased from old public-domain fox fables. They are useful precisely because `fox + other animal` does not reliably mean `predator + prey`.

### Fox and crow

A crow sat above the fox with food in its beak. The fox praised the crow until it opened its mouth to answer. The food fell; the fox took the food. The fox wanted what the crow carried, not necessarily the crow.

---

### Fox and stork

The fox served soup on a flat plate that the stork could not eat from properly. Later the stork returned the joke by serving food in a narrow-necked vessel that suited a beak but not a fox's muzzle. Both animals are diners; neither is dinner.

---

### Fox, cock, and dog

A fox tried to lure a cock down from a tree by pretending to be friendly. The cock directed the fox toward a dog hidden below. The attempted predator became the animal in immediate danger and fled.

---

### Fox and cat

A fox bragged to a cat about knowing many ways to escape danger. When hounds arrived, the cat climbed a tree while the fox hesitated among its many plans and was caught. The relation between fox and cat is comparison, not predation.

---

### Hare, tortoise, and fox

A hare and a tortoise agreed to race, and a fox chose the course and the finish. Here a fox appears in the same story as a hare without hunting it at all.

---

### Fox and hedgehog

A wounded fox lay exhausted while biting flies fed on it. A hedgehog offered to drive the flies away, and the fox refused because the present flies were already full and replacements might be hungrier. The fox is the thing being fed on.

---

### Fox and snake

A snake was swept downstream on a floating bundle of thorns. A fox watched from the bank and made a joke about the passenger matching the boat. No one eats anyone.

---

### Hares and foxes

Hares fighting eagles asked foxes to join them. The foxes declined because they knew both who the hares were and how dangerous the eagles were. `fox`, `hare`, and `eagle` denote political roles in the little fable, not a food chain.

---

### Eagle and fox

An eagle took a fox cub to feed its own young. The mother fox could not reach the nest. Later events allowed the fox to retaliate against the eagle's brood. A global rule saying that foxes are predators is already facing the wrong direction in the first half of the story.

---

### Fox and crab

A crab wandered away from the shore into a meadow. A hungry fox found it and ate it. This one really does look like a clean predator/prey substitution, but the habitat change is part of why the event occurs.

---

### Fox and goat

A fox trapped in a well persuaded a goat to climb down. The fox climbed onto the goat and escaped, leaving the goat behind. The goat is used as a step, not eaten.

---

### Fox and pheasants

A fox saw pheasants roosting above its reach and distracted them with strange movements until some fell from the branch. This is predation again, but the crucial operation is deception rather than a simple chase.

## Sentences that should make a checked substitution hesitate

The farmer said a fox ate the missing hen, but nobody saw what happened. There were fox tracks near the fence and raccoon tracks near the feed bin.

---

The fox did not eat the chicken. It chased the bird out of the weeds, and the farmer's dog caught it a few seconds later.

---

A fox chased a rabbit while an owl dropped onto a mouse beside the same hedgerow. The rabbit escaped and the owl flew away carrying the mouse.

---

Two foxes crossed the field. One stopped to eat berries. The other followed a rabbit into the brush, and the observer could not see what happened next.

---

The old hen had lost feathers before the fox entered the yard. Afterward the hen was gone. The sentence ordering tempts a causal reading that the observations do not prove.

---

A vixen brought food to three kits. From the road it looked like a rabbit, but the photograph was too blurred to distinguish a rabbit from a large rat.

---

One fox ate a mouse. Another fox watched from the far side of the creek. Later the second fox found the first fox's cache and stole what remained. Which fox should a transformation preserve as the actor?

---

The farmer kept hens, ducks, and two geese. A fox entered the enclosure and left carrying a bird. The witness could not tell which kind.

---

The fox approached the nest, but before it reached the eggs a crow carried one away. The fox then chased the crow and lost sight of the nest.

---

If the rabbit had run toward the open field, the fox probably would have caught it. Instead it doubled back through the brambles. Counterfactual prey is not actual prey.

---

In spring the fox mostly hunted voles near the ditch. By late summer it was eating fallen fruit and insects. A mapping that is defensible in one paragraph may not be defensible in the next.

---

A rabbit was found dead near the den. Fox tracks crossed the area, but so did tire tracks. `was eaten by a fox` would be an inference, not a substitution.

---

The story calls the same animal `the fox`, `the vixen`, `she`, and later `the mother`. A purely lexical replacement can change one name while leaving three aliases behind.

---

The fox caught a pullet, but the older hens stayed on the roost. A rule that knows only `chicken` may miss a perfectly ordinary chicken because the story uses a more specific word.

---

Mr. Fox invited Mrs. Stork to dinner. Here capitalization and anthropomorphic names matter: replacing every biological occurrence of `fox` and `stork` may still be syntactically easy while semantically turning a fable into nonsense.

## Why keep the failures

A useful transformation system should not be graded only on examples manufactured to make its declared correspondence come out true. These passages give several distinct failure modes:

- the proposed prey escapes;
- the fox eats something but did not kill it;
- the fox eats plant matter;
- another animal is the predator;
- the fox itself is prey or under attack;
- two animals interact without predation;
- the event is reported but uncertain;
- the event is negated or counterfactual;
- several possible prey species are left unresolved;
- one animal has several names in the same passage;
- a transformation preserves nouns but destroys the causal structure;
- an old fable assigns social or rhetorical roles rather than ecological ones.

That gives a dependently typed `sed` something more interesting to do than certify its own hand-picked examples. Sometimes the correct result should be a transformed passage; sometimes it should be a smaller transformed fragment; sometimes it should be a choice; and sometimes it should be a refusal with the failed assumption made explicit.

## Public-domain story sources worth mining further

These are useful sources for naturally varied fox language rather than more synthetic `fox ate chicken` sentences:

- [Joseph Jacobs, *The Fables of Aesop*, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/28) — includes Fox and Crow, Fox and Stork, Fox and Cat, Fox/Cock/Dog, Fox and Mosquitoes, Fox and Goat, and others.
- [*The Aesop for Children*, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/19994) — includes Dog/Cock/Fox, Fox and Pheasants, Fox and Crab, Fox and Hedgehog, Fox and Crow, Fox and Goat, and more.
- [George Fyler Townsend, *Three Hundred Aesop's Fables*, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/21) — a larger source with Hares and Foxes, Fox and Hedgehog, Crab and Fox, Fox and Crane, and many other combinations.
- [Grimm, *The Fox and the Cat*, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2591/2591-h/2591-h.htm) — especially useful because the fox/cat relation is about competing strategies under attack by hounds.
- [*Reynard the Fox*, Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/23026860/) — a much longer public-domain narrative with fox relations that are social, deceptive, adversarial, familial, and predatory rather than one repeated template.

## Natural-history anchors

The synthetic ecological passages above are deliberately plausible rather than claims about one observed fox. For the range of food and behavior, useful anchors include:

- [US Forest Service: red fox](https://research.fs.usda.gov/feis/species-reviews/vuvu) — small mammals, rabbits/hares, birds and eggs, insects, fruit, carrion, and other foods, with seasonal variation.
- [Indiana DNR: red fox](https://www.in.gov/dnr/fish-and-wildlife/wildlife-resources/animals/red-fox/) — rabbits, squirrels, mice, songbirds, fruit, vegetation, insects, carrion, and poultry.
- [University of Michigan BioKIDS: red fox](https://biokids.umich.edu/critters/Vulpes_vulpes/) — includes the characteristic mouse-hunting pounce, caching behavior, and the fact that foxes themselves have predators.
